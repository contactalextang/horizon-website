## [Best Practices for TLS Certificates on Internal Services Using DNS-01](https://tuxnet.dev/posts/tls-for-internal-services/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The article provides guidance on issuing TLS certificates for internal services via DNS-01 ACME validation, recommending against split-horizon DNS to simplify certificate management. Proper TLS for internal services reduces man-in-the-middle risks and aligns with zero-trust networking, while avoiding split-horizon DNS cuts operational overhead. It recommends using Let's Encrypt with DNS-01 challenges, wildcard certificates to limit name exposure in Certificate Transparency logs, and a central reverse proxy for TLS termination.

hackernews · Hacker News RSS · Jul 9, 14:57 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48846995)

**Background**: TLS certificates encrypt communications between clients and services, and are traditionally issued for public domains using ACME protocols like Let's Encrypt. DNS-01 validation proves domain ownership by placing a token in a DNS TXT record, enabling certificate issuance for services without public web access. Split-horizon DNS serves different DNS records to internal versus external clients, often creating complexity when mirroring public records internally. Avoiding split-horizon DNS simplifies network architecture while still securing internal services with publicly trusted certificates.

During the rewrite, the team confronted a high proportion of use‑free, double‑free and missing‑free bugs, which Rust’s ownership model and Drop trait turn into compile‑time errors; the process consumed roughly 5.9 billion input tokens, 690 million output tokens and 72 billion cached reads, costing about $165 k at API prices.

Rust provides memory safety through ownership, borrowing and RAII‑style Drop, making it attractive for projects that struggle with the mix of garbage‑collected objects and manually managed memory.

## [No leap second to be added at end of December 2026](https://datacenter.iers.org/data/latestVersion/bulletinC.txt) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) announced that no leap second will be inserted at the end of December 2026, keeping the UTC‑TAI offset at ‑37 seconds. This decision affects software and systems that depend on precise timekeeping, such as financial trading, telecommunications, and GPS, because the UTC‑TAI offset will remain unchanged, avoiding potential disruptions from a leap second insertion. With no leap second, the UTC‑TAI offset stays at ‑37 seconds, which also means the UTC‑GPS offset remains at ‑18 seconds given the constant 19‑second TAI‑GPS offset.

hackernews · Hacker News RSS · Jul 9, 14:16 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48846281)

**Background**: Leap seconds are occasionally added to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) to keep it aligned with Earth's irregular rotation, which is monitored by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS). The IERS predicts Earth orientation parameters and announces leap seconds only about six months in advance because the planet's rotation speed varies unpredictably due to geological, atmospheric, and oceanic processes. As of January 2026, UTC is exactly 37 seconds behind International Atomic Time (TAI), a offset that has remained unchanged since the last leap second in January 2017.
